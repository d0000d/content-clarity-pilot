The writing exercise

One page. A defined editorial problem.

  • Identify vague wording and information a reader would need to understand a service.
  • Explore a headline, introduction, service description, and next-step explanation in up to 600 words.
  • Write five illustrative FAQ answers with assumptions clearly labelled.
  • Review the draft for unsupported claims, unclear instructions, and unnecessary detail.

These are demonstrations of content structure. Real-world wording would require confirmed facts and approval from the relevant business.

About this project

Make the reasoning visible.

Each example should help a reader see what changed and why. The aim is a useful explanation rather than a claim about conversion, rankings, or commercial performance.

No services are ordered or paid for here. The project contains original portfolio exercises, not customer case studies or testimonials.

Before and after

From a generic welcome to a useful explanation.

This original demonstration uses a fictional bicycle repair shop, “Pedal Workshop.” It is a writing exercise, not a real business, client project, or evidence of commercial results. All services and policies below are illustrative.

Before · fictional starting text
“Welcome to our website. We provide a wide range of repairs with great service and competitive prices. Get in touch to find out more.”

The reader still needs to know what can be repaired, how a repair is agreed, and what information would help explain the problem.

After · demonstration copy

City-bike repairs, agreed before the work starts.

We check brakes, gears, and tyres, then explain the recommended work and proposed price for your approval.

To discuss an inspection, describe your bike type and the problem, with a useful photo if available. We first check whether the repair fits the service before suggesting an available appointment.

This is fictional sample copy. A real page would require confirmed services, approval procedures, and a contact channel.

Fictional FAQ exercise

Five answers a reader might need.

These answers belong only to the invented Pedal Workshop demonstration. They do not describe a real service or offer a repair.

How would I arrange an inspection?

In this fictional example, inspections require an appointment. An initial enquiry would include the bicycle type and a short description of the problem; an available appointment would then be confirmed.

Would work start without my approval?

No. The imagined process requires approval of the proposed work and price before repairs begin. Additional work would need a separate confirmation.

How would the price be determined?

A proposed price would follow an inspection of the bicycle. The example does not assume a fixed repair price before the fault is understood.

Would replacement parts be available?

Compatibility and availability would need to be checked for the particular bicycle. Parts would not be promised before that check.

How long would a repair take?

A proposed completion time would be confirmed after inspection and a parts check. This example makes no fixed turnaround promise.

Editorial approach

Define the question. Explain the process. Check the claims.

01 / Understand

Find the reader’s question.

What does the service do, who is it for, and what would someone need to understand before taking the next step?

02 / Explain

Replace generalities with details.

Describe a useful process, mark assumptions, and make the next step clear without inventing credentials or results.

03 / Review

Challenge the wording.

Check whether the sample answers real questions, avoids unsupported promises, and stays readable without losing necessary detail.
